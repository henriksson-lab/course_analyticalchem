Exercise: error and uncertainty

Goal

Distinguish precision, bias, reading SD, uncertainty in a mean and uncertainty in a derived analytical result. Calculate a small independent-input budget and explain why shared effects do not disappear when readings are repeated.

Background

Repeated readings of one stable preparation can reveal reading repeatability. They do not by themselves reveal sampling, extraction loss, stock uncertainty or shared volume effects. For independent normal readings, \(s/\sqrt n\) estimates the reading contribution to uncertainty in their mean. Standard uncertainty is on an SD-like scale; it is not automatically a 95% half-width.

For \(c=c_sV_a/V_f\) and small independent input uncertainties,

\[u(c)=c\sqrt{[u(c_s)/c_s]^2+[u(V_a)/V_a]^2+[u(V_f)/V_f]^2}.\]

Correlated inputs require covariance terms. A known significant bias calls for investigation or a justified correction with its uncertainty, rather than an arbitrary extra error bar.

By hand

B1. A reference is 10.00 mg/L. Method A has mean 10.00 and SD 0.02 mg/L; B has mean 10.50 and the same SD. Compare precision and estimated bias. Here reference uncertainty is neglected. Hint

B2. Five independent stable readings have mean 10.00 and SD 0.3162 mg/L. Calculate the reading uncertainty in the mean. Which quantity still has SD 0.3162 mg/L? Hint

B3. A 100.0 mg/L stock has standard uncertainty 0.4 mg/L. A 5.000 mL aliquot has standard uncertainty 0.006 mL; final volume 50.00 mL has standard uncertainty 0.03 mL. Calculate final concentration, each output-scale contribution and their combined standard uncertainty. Hint

B4. Reading noise contributes \(0.20/\sqrt n\) mg/L and an independent shared component is 0.10 mg/L. Find combined uncertainty at \(n=1\) and \(n=100\), and its limit as n grows. Hint

B5. A report gives “10.00 ± 0.04 mg/L” without defining ±. Name two possible meanings and the information needed to distinguish them. Hint

Explore

The first graph shows readings of one prepared reference solution. The second shows its model uncertainty in the mean. A new run gives a new common offset; adding readings in the same run preserves that offset and existing readings.

E1. Keep reading SD 0.20, shared uncertainty 0.10 and bias 0.30 mg/L. Compare 1, 10 and 100 readings without changing run. Which plotted uncertainty approaches zero, and which does not? Hint

E2. Set reading SD to zero but retain shared uncertainty and known bias. What does the observed SD imply about the full result? Hint

E3. Apply the supplied exact known-bias correction. Which offset disappears? Why can the mean still differ from the known reference in one run? Hint

E4. With many readings, change the preparation/run repeatedly. Explain why this probes something different from more readings of the same preparation. Hint

In R

Run from the supplied project root. The reference-material table contains repeat readings of one material by each method, not independent collected waters.

            sd_mg_l = sd(measured_mg_l),
            u_mean_mg_l = sd_mg_l / sqrt(n),
            bias_mg_l = mean_mg_l - first(reference_mg_l), .groups = "drop")
summary_precision
ggplot(measurements, aes(measured_mg_l, method)) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 10, linetype = "dashed") + geom_point() +
  labs(x = "Measured concentration (mg/L)", y = "Method") + theme_minimal()

                   aliquot = c_final * .006 / 5,

write_csv(summary_precision, "output/precision-summary.csv")
write_csv(data.frame(source = names(contributions),
                    u_mg_l = as.numeric(contributions)), "output/dilution-budget.csv")

Hints

Hint B1

Precision concerns repeated values; estimated bias compares mean with a suitable reference. They answer different questions.

Hint B2

Divide SD by \(\sqrt5\) for the reading contribution to the mean. The individual reading distribution retains its own spread.

Hint B3

Final concentration is 10 mg/L. Convert each relative input uncertainty to an output-scale contribution before adding its square.

Hint B4

Combine the squared reading and shared components. Only the independent reading term is divided by n.

Hint B5

It could denote a standard uncertainty, expanded uncertainty with a stated coverage factor, or a confidence-interval half-width under a sampling model.

Hints E1–E4

Track reading noise, common random offset and deliberately known fixed bias separately. The bias correction is exact only by this toy model’s assumption; real corrections can have their own uncertainty.

Hint R1

Compare both bias_mg_l and sd_mg_l. A single accidentally close mean is not full validation.

Hint R2

Change only the stock contribution from 0.040 to 0.020 mg/L. Keep the two volume contributions.

Hint R3

sqrt() is applied to the sum of variances. A known uncorrected bias is not being treated as an extra random term in this illustrative curve.

Hint R4

sd() describes spread among readings. The supplied uncertainty includes a component shared by those readings, which their scatter cannot estimate.

Solutions

Solutions B1–B5

B1. A and B have equal repeat precision under the stated conditions. A has estimated bias zero in this series; B has +0.50 mg/L. Equal precision does not imply equal closeness to the reference, and this neglects reference uncertainty only for the example.

B2. \(u_{reading}(\bar x)=0.3162/\sqrt5\approx0.1414\) mg/L. Individual readings retain SD 0.3162 mg/L; the mean has a smaller reading component. Neither number establishes sampling, recovery or common-bias uncertainty.

B3. \(c=100(5/50)=10\) mg/L. Contributions are 0.040, 0.012 and 0.006 mg/L. Their squared sum is 0.001780 (mg/L)², so \(u(c)=0.04219\) mg/L. The calculation assumes a valid model, small uncertainties and independent inputs.

B4. At n=1, \(u=\sqrt{0.20^2+0.10^2}=0.22361\) mg/L. At n=100, \(u=\sqrt{0.20^2/100+0.10^2}=0.10198\) mg/L. It approaches 0.10 mg/L, because repeated readings do not create new information about the common effect.

B5. If 0.04 is a standard uncertainty, no 95% coverage is implied by itself. If it is expanded uncertainty, give k and its coverage rationale. If it is a confidence-interval half-width, give the sampling/model construction and level. Without that information the reader cannot interpret the uncertainty claim.

Solutions E1–E4

E1. The reading-only component is 0.20, 0.06325 and 0.0200 mg/L at n=1,10,100; combined values are about 0.22361, 0.11832 and 0.10198 mg/L. The mean can remain displaced because bias and the common offset do not shrink within the run.

E2. The readings coincide and their observed SD is zero for n≥2. The known bias and realised shared offset remain. Zero observed repeatability scatter therefore does not establish an exact analytical result.

E3. Subtracting the specified known bias removes the fixed 0.30 mg/L effect. The common random offset remains, along with residual reading noise. The model assumes the correction exact; a real correction may add an uncertainty component.

E4. A new run draws a new common offset. Many readings within one preparation retain the same offset. Studying independent preparations can reveal sources that within-solution repeatability cannot, provided the design actually varies the relevant steps.

Solutions R1–R4

R1. B is precise but displaced. A/B both have SD about 0.01581 mg/L; C has SD 0.31623 mg/L. C’s mean is 10.00 in this small series, but that does not prove unbiased performance in different samples, matrices or conditions.

R2. New combined uncertainty is \(\sqrt{0.020^2+0.012^2+0.006^2}=0.02408\) mg/L. The unchanged volume terms prevent a simple factor-of-two change.

R3. The shared 0.10 mg/L component remains. The curve omits a known uncorrected bias rather than pretending it is random; that bias requires its own investigation or justified correction. The values are standard uncertainties, not 95% interval half-widths.

R4. R’s SD measures within-run reading spread and may differ from the supplied population reading SD in a finite sample. The model uncertainty concerns the mean and combines a shrinking independent reading contribution with a shared component. Changing a common offset moves all readings together without widening their scatter. At n=1, sample SD is undefined, not evidence of no variability.

Where this leads

Calibration and quality control adds uncertain slope and intercept when a response is converted to concentration. Ask which uncertainty components and validity checks accompany every later reported result.
