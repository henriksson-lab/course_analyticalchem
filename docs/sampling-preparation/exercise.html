Exercise: sampling and preparation

Goal

Distinguish representative sampling from precise repeated measurement; reverse an extraction and dilution chain; calculate an ideal partition recovery; and state which losses an added spike can test. Work by hand before using the demo and R. All supplied datasets are simulated.

Background

A heterogeneous solid has three equal-mass strata with mean iron concentrations 8, 14 and 25 mg/kg. Its bulk mass mean weights the strata by mass, not by the number of convenient grabs. A composite can estimate a bulk quantity but no longer preserves every original spatial difference.

For a caffeine extraction, the concentration in the measured flask must be returned through aliquot and extract volumes to the original mass:

\[w_{extracted}=c_{final}\frac{V_{final}}{V_{aliquot}} \frac{V_{extract}}{m_{sample}}.\]

Use litres for \(V_{extract}\) when \(c\) is mg/L. The result is extracted caffeine; complete native-analyte recovery is not established. For ideal immiscible phases, \(D=c_o/c_a\) and the fraction remaining after n equal fresh extractions using total solvent \(V_o\) is \([V_a/(V_a+D V_o/n)]^n\).

By hand

B1. Find the bulk mean of the three equal-mass strata. What mean would many top-only grabs approach? Explain why a small SD would not fix this difference. Hint

B2. Instead suppose top, middle and bottom represent mass fractions 0.50, 0.30 and 0.20. Calculate the bulk mean. Why is an equal-stratum average now wrong? Hint

B3. A 0.5000 g dry portion gives 25.00 mL extract. A 2.00 mL aliquot made to 10.00 mL reads 1.92 mg caffeine/L. Calculate extract concentration, extracted amount, and extracted caffeine in mg/g. Hint

B4. At \(D=9\) with 20 mL aqueous phase, compare recovery using one 10 mL extraction with two fresh 5 mL extractions. State the physical assumptions. Hint

B5. A spike added after extraction gives the expected incremental response. Does this establish complete recovery of native bound analyte? Explain which parts of the measurement it did and did not experience. Hint

Explore

The first experiment separates sampling allocation from the mass-weighted quantity. The second isolates ideal equilibrium recovery at fixed total solvent. Predict before each change; the known target is visible only because this is a simulation.

E1. Keep three grabs per stratum, then change top grabs to 20 while retaining three middle and three bottom grabs. Compare the naive all-grab mean and the mean of stratum means. Which weighting targets the equal-mass bulk quantity? Hint

E2. Set middle and bottom grabs to zero. What becomes unestimable? Repeat the sampling run, and explain why more top-only observations do not solve it. Hint

E3. Restore all three strata, then change the sampling run without changing the target. What varies randomly and what stays defined by the sampling question? Hint

E4. Select successive extraction. At \(D=9\), 20 mL aqueous and 10 mL total solvent, compare one and two extractions. Then lower D. Why does dividing solvent not guarantee complete real extraction? Hint

In R

Run from the course project’s root. The raw dictionaries define sample and preparation identities. In the grab table, rows are grabs within a specified position. T1a and T1b are independent preparations of the same supplied material, not independently collected materials.

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
grabs <- read_csv("data/course/sampling.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
strata <- grabs |>
  group_by(position) |>
  summarise(n_grabs = n(), mean_mg_kg = mean(iron_mg_kg), .groups = "drop")
strata
mean(strata$mean_mg_kg)
prep <- read_csv("data/course/extractions.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
stopifnot(!anyDuplicated(prep$preparation_id), all(prep$mass_g > 0))
results <- prep |>
  mutate(extract_mg_l = final_caffeine_mg_l * final_ml / aliquot_ml,
         extracted_mg = extract_mg_l * extract_ml / 1000,
         caffeine_mg_g = extracted_mg / mass_g)
results
fig <- ggplot(results, aes(preparation_id, caffeine_mg_g)) + geom_point() +
  labs(x = "Independent preparation", y = "Extracted caffeine (mg/g)") +
  theme_minimal()
fig
dir.create("output", showWarnings = FALSE)
write_csv(results, "output/extracted-caffeine.csv")
one_recovery <- 1 - 20 / (20 + 9 * 10)
two_recovery <- 1 - (20 / (20 + 9 * 5))^2
100 * c(one = one_recovery, two = two_recovery)

R1. Confirm B1, B3 and B4. Explain why mean(grabs$iron_mg_kg) happens to give the same bulk result for this particular balanced table but is not a universal sampling rule. Hint

R2. Explain the unit conversion /1000 and name the material basis of each new results column. Which result would be wrong if you used measured final concentration directly as mg/g? Hint

R3. A new preparation uses 0.4000 g, 40.00 mL extract, and 1.00 mL made to 10.00 mL giving 1.50 mg/L. Add a calculation to your report and explain why the result still needs a recovery qualification. Hint

R4. Export the sampling experiment and save sampling-demo.csv at the project root. Reproduce both averages when all strata are represented:

Why does an absent stratum need to remain missing rather than being assigned 0 mg/kg? Hint

Hints

Hint B1

For equal masses, average stratum means. Repeating a biased selection changes random scatter but not the material left unsampled.

Hint B2

Multiply each concentration by its material mass fraction, then sum. Fractions must refer to the intended target and add to one.

Hint B3

Undo the final dilution first, multiply by the total extract in litres, then divide the extracted mg by original grams.

Hint B4

For one extraction, the fraction left is \(20/(20+9\times10)\). For two, multiply the fraction left after each fresh 5 mL extraction.

Hint B5

A late spike does not pass through earlier losses. Even an early freely dissolved spike may not behave like native bound material.

Hints E1–E4

Changing sample allocation changes the weighting of a pooled average. In the extraction experiment, keep total solvent fixed; every portion is fresh, phases equilibrate and are separated completely in this ideal model.

Hint R1

The raw table contains four grabs from each equal-mass stratum. Balanced counts make the arithmetic coincide. Unequal counts generally break that coincidence.

Hint R2

mg/L × L gives mg, then mg/g uses original sample grams. The final flask and original solid do not have interchangeable concentration units.

Hint R3

Use \((1.50\times10/1)\times0.04000/0.4000\) and identify which losses are assumed absent. The formula alone does not demonstrate them absent.

Hint R4

group_by() separates stratum summaries. An unsampled stratum has unknown concentration, not a measured zero. A bulk estimate needs its evidence or a justified model.

Solutions

Solutions B1–B5

B1. \((8+14+25)/3=15.667\) mg/kg. Top-only sampling approaches 8 mg/kg. A small repeat SD describes the top measurements, not the bias from excluding other material.

B2. \(0.50(8)+0.30(14)+0.20(25)=13.2\) mg/kg. Equal weights would represent three equal masses that no longer exist in this new target.

B3. Final-to-extract factor \(10/2=5\) gives 9.60 mg/L extract. Amount is \(9.60\times0.02500=0.240\) mg; divided by 0.5000 g it gives 0.480 mg/g extracted.

B4. One recovery \(1-20/110=0.81818\); two recoveries together give \(1-(20/65)^2=0.90533\). This assumes constant distribution ratio and phase volumes, equilibrium, complete separation and fresh solvent for each extraction, with no reaction or handling loss. It is not validation of real native extraction.

B5. No. The late spike tests incremental behaviour after it was added, not native analyte lost before addition. A native bound analyte can differ from a freely dissolved spike, so equivalence also needs evidence.

Solutions E1–E4

E1. With equal grab counts, the two averages agree. Twenty top plus three middle and three bottom grabs make the naive average target \((20\times8+3\times14+3\times25)/26=10.654\) mg/kg in expectation. The average of the three stratum means remains near 15.667 mg/kg and uses the supplied mass weights. Exact simulated results vary with run.

E2. The full bulk quantity cannot be estimated from all strata by this procedure because middle and bottom are absent. Top-only precision cannot supply their concentrations. The demo withholds the stratified estimate rather than inventing zeros.

E3. The realised grabs and estimated means vary. The defined equal-mass target and the rule for weighting strata remain fixed. More independent grabs reduce random variation under this model but do not justify a different sampling target.

E4. Recoveries are 81.8% and 90.5%. Lower D leaves more analyte in the aqueous phase. Several fresh extractions improve this ideal equilibrium recovery, but bound analyte, reaction, emulsion, incomplete equilibration or handling loss can invalidate the ideal assumptions.

Solutions R1–R4

R1. Supplied stratum means are 8, 14 and 25 mg/kg. Equal counts and equal stratum masses make the pooled mean equal the equal-stratum mean. The three caffeine results are 0.480, 0.490 and 0.750 mg/g. Recovery calculations match B4.

R2. /1000 converts extract mL into L. extract_mg_l refers to the extract; extracted_mg is amount in its total volume; caffeine_mg_g refers to original solid mass. Reporting 1.92 mg/L final as 1.92 mg/g confuses quantities and ignores the preparation chain.

R3. \(1.50\times10=15.0\) mg/L extract; \(15.0\times0.04000=0.600\) mg; \(0.600/0.4000=1.50\) mg/g extracted. This still assumes valid measured concentration and no transfer loss, and does not establish complete native recovery.

R4. R reproduces the demo’s all-grab mean and equal mean of stratum means when all three strata appear. Missing rows for a stratum mean it was not sampled. Replacing that missing evidence with zero would create a false bulk estimate.

Where this leads

Error and uncertainty separates variability from bias and asks which uncertainty sources a set of repeat readings can actually estimate. In later methods, keep the original material basis when undoing aliquot, extract and dilution steps.
