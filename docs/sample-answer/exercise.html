Exercise: the analytical question

Goal

By the end you should be able to define a chemical measurand, distinguish concentration from amount, show individual analytical observations, and explain what a sample comparison supports. Do the small calculations on paper before R.

Background

A measurand names the intended quantity, material and conditions. Here it is chloride mass concentration in supplied collected water, in mg chloride/L. The analyte is chloride; the matrix contains water and other species. A measured signal becomes this quantity only through a suitable chemical measurement model.

For a homogeneous solution, \(c=m/V\) and \(m=cV\). If no analyte is lost or added, dilution gives \(c_f=c_oV_a/V_f\). Final volume means the total after preparation, including solvent and reagents. The collected material, aliquot and final flask are different stages; name the stage in a reported concentration.

By hand

B1. The analysis establishes 0.0750 mg chloride in a 25.00 mL portion. Calculate concentration in mg/L. What information would still be needed to claim a location-wide mean? Hint

B2. A homogeneous solution is 20.0 mg/L. Calculate the analyte amount in (a) 0.250 L and (b) 0.500 L. Does its concentration change when a larger portion is taken? Hint

B3. A 10.00 mL aliquot is made to 50.00 mL total. The final concentration is 0.800 mg/L. Find the original concentration and analyte amount in the aliquot. Hint

B4. Four inlet samples give 18, 20, 19 and 21 mg/L; four outlet samples give 12, 13, 11 and 14 mg/L. Calculate both means and their difference. State one supported descriptive claim and one unsupported causal claim. Hint

B5. A colleague proposes conductivity as a direct chloride concentration measurement in a new water matrix. What chemical relation is missing? Would a perfectly reproducible R report establish it? Hint

Explore

Predict whether each action changes concentration, amount, or both before using the demo. The solution is homogeneous and the solvent adds no chloride. There is no reaction, adsorption or transfer loss. Model inputs are exact, not a supplied uncertainty budget.

E1. At the defaults (20 mg/L original, 250 mL aliquot, twofold dilution), select each reporting quantity. Give the value, unit and material stage for all three. Hint

E2. Keep concentration and dilution factor fixed; double aliquot volume from 250 to 500 mL. Explain what changes and why the plotted final concentration does not. Hint

E3. Restore 250 mL, then increase dilution factor from 2 to 5. What is conserved? Explain why multiplying the final concentration by 5 recovers the original value. Set original concentration to zero and repeat. Hint

E4. Export the CSV. Which column changes between the original aliquot and final flask? Which equality should both rows satisfy in R? Hint

In R

Open the supplied R project and run these commands from its root, where data/ is located. Keep raw data unchanged. The dataset is simulated and one row represents one independently collected water sample, not a repeated reading.

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
water <- read_csv("data/course/water.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
stopifnot(!anyDuplicated(water$sample_id), !anyNA(water$chloride_mg_l),
          all(water$collected_l > 0))
summary_water <- water |>
  group_by(location) |>
  summarise(n_samples = n(), mean_mg_l = mean(chloride_mg_l),
            sd_mg_l = sd(chloride_mg_l), .groups = "drop")
summary_water
water <- water |>
  mutate(chloride_g_l = chloride_mg_l / 1000,
         chloride_mg = chloride_mg_l * collected_l)
fig <- ggplot(water, aes(location, chloride_mg_l)) +
  geom_point(position = position_jitter(width = .08, height = 0, seed = 7)) +
  labs(x = "Sampling location", y = "Chloride (mg/L)") + theme_minimal()
fig
dir.create("output", showWarnings = FALSE)
write_csv(water, "output/water-derived.csv")
ggsave("output/water-observations.png", plot = fig, width = 7, height = 4)

R1. Check B4 and report both sample counts. Does the row order establish before/after pairs? Hint

R2. Inspect chloride_g_l and chloride_mg. For W2, explain why the values are 0.020 g/L and 5.00 mg, with different units and meanings. Hint

R3. Write a short result paragraph using the observations and means, then name the sampling information absent from a location-wide claim. Hint

R4. Save the demo export next to the R project, then verify mass conservation. This guarded block does nothing until that optional file exists:

if (file.exists("concentration-and-amount.csv")) {
  demo <- read_csv("concentration-and-amount.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
  demo <- demo |>
    mutate(recomputed_mg = concentration_mg_l * volume_ml / 1000)
  print(demo)
  stopifnot(all(abs(demo$recomputed_mg - demo$analyte_mg) < 1e-8))
}

Which physical failure would make the demo’s conservation assumption unsuitable for a real preparation? Hint

Hints

Hint B1

Convert millilitres to litres before dividing. One portion supplies no sampling design for an entire location or time period.

Hint B2

Use \(m=cV\) with litres. The intensive concentration remains fixed when a larger portion of a homogeneous solution is taken.

Hint B3

The preparation factor is final total volume divided by aliquot volume. Calculate amount either before or after dilution; it should agree.

Hint B4

Average each set separately. The observations are from collected waters; they do not establish a treatment assignment, matching or representative temporal design.

Hint B5

Conductivity responds to several mobile ionic species. Reproducing a calculation does not establish that its chemical model is selective enough.

Hints E1–E4

Track \(c\), \(V\) and \(m\) separately. Changing aliquot volume at fixed dilution factor changes both original and final volumes proportionally. Adding solvent at fixed aliquot does not add analyte.

Hint R1

summarise() returns one summary per location after group_by(). Matching is a design property, not a coincidence of table positions.

Hint R2

Dividing mg/L by 1000 changes to g/L; multiplying mg/L by collected L gives mg.

Hint R3

Say what was observed, how many collected samples support it, and which place and time scope is actually given. Do not invent representativeness.

Hint R4

The /1000 converts mL to L. Consider transfer loss, adsorption, reaction or analyte in the added solvent.

Solutions

Solutions B1–B5

B1. \(0.0750/0.02500=3.00\) mg/L. A location-wide mean also needs a sampling design defining the location/time population and how portions represent it; the amount calculation supplies neither that design nor measurement uncertainty.

B2. \(20.0\times0.250=5.00\) mg and \(20.0\times0.500=10.0\) mg. Concentration is still 20.0 mg/L. Taking more solution changes collected amount, not the composition of a homogeneous solution.

B3. Factor \(50.00/10.00=5\); original \(0.800\times5=4.00\) mg/L. The aliquot contains \(4.00\times0.01000=0.0400\) mg, equal to \(0.800\times0.05000\) mg in the final flask. “Add 50 mL solvent” would describe a different volume.

B4. Inlet mean \((18+20+19+21)/4=19.5\) mg/L; outlet mean \((12+13+11+14)/4=12.5\) mg/L. Difference 7.0 mg/L. The sampled outlet waters have a lower observed mean. The data do not establish that a treatment caused this change, or that every outlet sample at another time would be lower.

B5. A justified relation between conductivity and chloride in the proposed matrix is missing. Other ions can alter conductivity without the same chloride change. A reproducible wrong model remains wrong; R does not establish selectivity.

Solutions E1–E4

E1. Original 20 mg/L; original aliquot amount 5 mg; final concentration 10 mg/L in 500 mL. Each statement names a different quantity or stage.

E2. A 500 mL aliquot contains 10 mg and becomes 1000 mL at factor 2. Final concentration remains 10 mg/L because amount and final volume doubled together.

E3. At 250 mL and factor 5 the final volume is 1250 mL, final concentration 4 mg/L and amount still 5 mg. \(4\times5=20\) mg/L returns to the original basis. At zero original chloride, amount and final concentration remain zero for every volume; missing evidence in a real sample would not justify entering zero.

E4. concentration_mg_l and volume_ml change; analyte_mg is conserved. Each row satisfies \(m=cV/1000\) when V is in mL. The ideal equality assumes no chemical or transfer change.

Solutions R1–R4

R1. Both locations have four collected samples; means match B4. Their sample SDs are about 1.291 mg/L. No matching variable or paired design is supplied; row order cannot create pairs.

R2. W2 is 20 mg/L, equivalent to 0.020 g/L. Its 0.250 L portion contains 5.00 mg. The first is concentration with a changed mass unit; the second is amount in a specified portion.

R3. One suitable paragraph is: “The four sampled inlet waters averaged 19.5 mg chloride/L and the four outlet waters 12.5 mg/L, an observed difference of 7.0 mg/L. Individual observations retain within-location spread. The supplied design does not establish causation or a broader time/location population mean.”

R4. The exported values reproduce the conservation equation up to floating- point rounding. Adsorption to a vessel, incomplete transfer or chemical loss could break it; contaminated solvent could add analyte. A small computational rounding tolerance is not an analytical acceptance criterion.

Where this leads

Sampling and preparation asks whether the aliquot represents the intended material and whether analyte recovery is complete. Keep sample identity, material scope and units through every later calibration and instrument result.
