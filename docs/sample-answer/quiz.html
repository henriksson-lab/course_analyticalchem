Quiz: the analytical question

Try each question before opening its hint or answer. These practice questions check chemical reasoning and small calculations; they are not a graded question bank.

Q1. A report says “iron = 4.2”. Give four pieces of information needed before this is an interpretable analytical result.

Hint Q1

Name the chemical quantity, material, unit and relevant preparation conditions.

Answer Q1

Specify which iron quantity (for example dissolved iron after a defined reduction), the sampled material and scope, the unit and concentration or amount basis, and the preparation/measurement conditions. “Iron” alone does not distinguish original Fe(II), dissolved total iron and particulate material; 4.2 alone has no unit or denominator.

Q2. A homogeneous solution is 12.0 mg chloride/L. How much chloride is in 400 mL? What is its concentration in g/L?

Hint Q2

Use litres for amount; use the mg-to-g conversion separately for concentration.

Answer Q2

\(m=12.0\times0.400=4.80\) mg. Concentration is \(12.0/1000=0.0120\) g/L. Taking a portion does not change a homogeneous solution’s concentration; amount and concentration have different meanings.

Q3. A 2.00 mL aliquot is made to 20.00 mL total and measured as 0.300 mg/L. What was the original concentration?

Hint Q3

Reverse the final-to-aliquot volume factor.

Answer Q3

The factor is \(20.00/2.00=10\), so original concentration is 3.00 mg/L, conditional on no analyte loss or addition. Adding 20 mL solvent would produce a different final volume from making up to 20 mL.

Q4. Why is “mg/L = ppm” not a universal unit definition?

Hint Q4

Compare a mass-per-volume quantity with a mass fraction.

Answer Q4

mg/L has a volume denominator; a mass fraction needs a mass denominator. For a sufficiently dilute water solution they can be approximately related through density, but this is an assumption, not a definition that applies to every material.

Q5. Two flasks have the same calibrated final concentration. Can their original samples have different concentrations?

Hint Q5

Ask whether the preparation factors are equal.

Answer Q5

Yes. A final concentration of 1 mg/L after factors 5 and 20 corresponds to original values 5 and 20 mg/L respectively, if the preparation model is valid. A signal cannot replace the aliquot and total-volume record.

Q6. A table has four inlet rows followed by four outlet rows. Can row 1 be paired with row 5 for a before/after analysis?

Hint Q6

A pair is defined by experimental identity, not by table order.

Answer Q6

No paired design is supplied by those positions. A subject/sample identifier and the collection design must establish which observations are genuinely matched. Arbitrary row pairing invents evidence.

Q7. The concentration field is missing for an unsuccessful measurement. Is replacing it with zero a harmless way to calculate a mean?

Hint Q7

Distinguish absent measurement evidence from an observed zero.

Answer Q7

No. Zero is a numerical result; missing means the quantity was not established. Replacing missing values with zero can bias the mean. Document the failure and report how many valid results support a summary.

Q8. A report renders perfectly from a new R session. Does that prove its chemical conclusion?

Hint Q8

Which kinds of mistakes can be repeated exactly?

Answer Q8

It establishes useful computational reproducibility, but can repeat a wrong unit, unrepresentative sampling, incorrect preparation factor or nonspecific response. The chemical model and evidence still need review.
