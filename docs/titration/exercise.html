Exercise: equilibrium and titration

Goal

By hand and in R, calculate analyte amount from standardised titrant, explain why an observed endpoint only estimates equivalence, and report an EDTA result on an explicit CaCO₃ equivalent basis. Use a pH curve to reason about endpoint choice rather than memorising “stop at pH 7”.

Background

For the strong-acid reaction \(\mathrm{H^++OH^-\rightarrow H_2O}\), equivalence means equal reacted amounts. With 1:1 stoichiometry, \(c_a=c_bV_{eq}/V_a\). Read delivered burette volume as final minus initial, then subtract justified blank consumption on the same volume basis. A measured endpoint estimates \(V_{eq}\) under a stated indicator or numerical rule.

At 25 °C the ideal strong-acid/strong-base equivalence has pH 7. A weak acid’s conjugate base changes equivalence pH without changing the number of moles that must be neutralised. The supplied weak-acid comparison uses pKa 4.76.

For this simplified hardness procedure assume accessible Ca and Mg react selectively 1:1 with EDTA under the supplied conditions. Report the combined amount as CaCO₃ using 100.09 g/mol. This equivalent basis does not prove that carbonate was present or distinguish Ca from Mg. All course data are simulated.

By hand

B1. A 25.00 mL strong-acid aliquot reaches its estimated endpoint after 25.00 mL of standardised 0.1000 mol/L base. Calculate acid concentration and reacted amount. Show where litres are necessary. Hint

B2. The same initial amount is instead a weak monoprotic acid. Does its equivalence require less base? Must equivalence still occur at pH 7? Explain using stoichiometry and equilibrium separately. Hint

B3. A 50.00 mL water sample uses EDTA at 0.01000 mol/L. Burette readings are 0.10 and 6.30 mL; a matched blank consumes 0.05 mL. Calculate metal amount and hardness as mg/L CaCO₃. Predict the error direction if the blank is omitted. Hint

B4. An electrode reads pH 6.0 at 24.90 mL and 8.0 at 25.10 mL. Calculate the finite-difference pH slope and its assigned midpoint volume. Explain why using only the row difference is wrong if addition volumes vary. Hint

Explore

The embedded model fixes 25.00 mL of 0.1000 mol/L acid and solves charge balance, including water. The chosen pH trigger represents an idealised endpoint rule, not a real indicator transition range or validated algorithm.

E1. Predict what changes when switching strong to weak acid while holding base concentration fixed. Compare equivalence volume, equivalence pH and the volume found by the pH 7 trigger. Hint

E2. Change base from 0.100 to 0.050 mol/L. Predict the new equivalence volume before moving the control. Explain why “weaker acid means less volume” is the wrong reasoning for this comparison. Hint

E3. Keep weak acid, vary its pKa and choose a trigger pH away from its actual equivalence pH. Describe the endpoint bias and why extra decimal places would not fix it. Export one scenario and state what the model omits. Hint

In R

Open the supplied project at its root. The following workflow runs in a fresh R session and does not require a demo export. It uses your earlier read_csv, mutate and plotting skills, adding diff() for consecutive changes.

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
curve <- read_csv("data/course/acid-titration.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
slopes <- data.frame(
  midpoint_ml = (head(curve$volume_ml, -1) + tail(curve$volume_ml, -1)) / 2,
  ph_per_ml = diff(curve$ph) / diff(curve$volume_ml))
ggplot(curve, aes(volume_ml, ph)) + geom_line() +
  labs(x = "0.1000 mol/L base added (mL)", y = "pH") + theme_minimal()
ggplot(slopes, aes(midpoint_ml, ph_per_ml)) + geom_line() +
  labs(x = "Addition midpoint (mL)", y = "Finite-difference pH per mL") +
  theme_minimal()
acid_mol_l <- 0.1000 * 25.00 / 25.00
hardness <- read_csv("data/course/edta.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  mutate(delivered_ml = final_burette_ml - initial_burette_ml,
         corrected_ml = delivered_ml - blank_ml,
         moles_metal = edta_mol_l * corrected_ml / 1000,
         hardness_mg_l_CaCO3 = moles_metal * 100.09 * 1000 / (sample_ml / 1000))
hardness
write_csv(hardness, "output/titration-exercise.csv")

R1. Explain the units in each calculated column. Locate the steep region and explain why the slope maximum is an exploratory check rather than complete endpoint validation. Hint

R2. Compare H1a, H1b and H2a. Which are independent preparations of the same supplied sample, and which values may be described as separate waters? State one source of uncertainty not estimated by their difference. Hint

R3. Independently calculate a new result for 25.00 mL water: 4.10 mL delivered EDTA, 0.10 mL blank, 0.01000 mol/L titrant. Add the result and its correct unit to your report. Hint

Hints

B1: amount before concentration

Use \(n=cV\) with volume in litres, then divide by the original acid volume. Matching millilitre units may cancel only when you use a volume ratio.

B2: two different questions

Stoichiometry determines the amount of titrant; equilibrium determines species and pH. A conjugate base remains after neutralising a weak acid.

B3: delivered volume and reporting basis

First subtract initial from final, then the matched blank. EDTA mol equals accessible metal mol in this scenario. Convert to equivalent CaCO₃ mass and divide by the original sample volume.

B4: use actual spacing

Divide 2.0 pH units by the difference in millilitres. The midpoint is the mean of the two added volumes.

E1: compare volume and pH separately

Read both equivalence outputs before inspecting the chosen endpoint. The initial acid amount is held fixed by the model.

E2: dilute titrant, fixed moles

Halving titrant concentration requires twice its volume for the same moles.

E3: endpoint bias is not reading precision

Compare the amount inferred at the trigger with the known model amount. The simulation excludes noise, incomplete mixing and instrument/junction effects.

R1: read diff

diff gives consecutive differences. Unequal volume steps require their own denominators; differentiation amplifies noise.

R2: inspect sample_id

Preparation IDs are not the same as independent collection IDs. Titrant standardisation or endpoint bias can be common to several preparations.

R3: reuse the model with a new denominator

The corrected volume is 4.00 mL, but this aliquot is 25.00 mL rather than 50.00 mL.

Solutions

Full worked solutions

B1. Base amount is \(0.1000\times0.02500=0.002500\) mol. The 1:1 reaction therefore consumed 0.002500 mol acid. Dividing by 0.02500 L acid gives 0.1000 mol/L. Multiplying concentration by 25.00 without converting mL to L would be a thousand-fold amount error.

B2. With the same monoprotic acid amount, the same base moles are required, so equivalence volume remains 25.00 mL at the same base concentration. A weak acid leaves its conjugate base at equivalence, and its pH can exceed 7. Acid strength changes the pH curve, not the supplied number of acid moles.

B3. Delivered volume \(6.30-0.10=6.20\) mL; corrected volume \(6.20-0.05=6.15\) mL. Metal amount is \(0.01000\times0.00615=6.15\times10^{-5}\) mol. Equivalent CaCO₃ mass is \(6.15\times10^{-5}\times100.09\times1000=6.155535\) mg. Divide by 0.05000 L to obtain 123.1107 mg/L as CaCO₃. Omitting positive blank consumption biases this estimate high. It is not a separate calcium result.

B4. \((8.0-6.0)/(25.10-24.90)=10.0\) pH units per mL, assigned to 25.00 mL. The denominator represents actual added volume. A difference per row silently assumes equal step sizes and can distort the inferred steep region.

E1. Equivalence volume stays fixed while weak-acid equivalence pH rises. The pH 7 trigger is reached before weak-acid equivalence and underestimates the acid amount in this model. No general rule sets every endpoint to pH 7.

E2. Required base moles remain 2.500 mmol. At 0.0500 mol/L, equivalence volume is \(0.002500/0.0500=0.05000\) L = 50.00 mL for either acid model. The changed base concentration, not acid strength, caused the volume change.

E3. A trigger below equivalence pH usually stops early and gives low inferred acid; one above it stops late and gives high inferred acid. The precise volume depends on pKa and trigger. This is a systematic endpoint-rule problem, not a lack of printed precision. If the selected trigger is already outside the displayed titration range, it is unavailable rather than evidence of zero acid. The ideal model does not supply real electrode, volume, mixing or recovery uncertainty.

R1. Delivered/corrected volumes are mL, metal amount mol, hardness mg/L as CaCO₃. The slope plot locates a steep region near 25 mL; its value depends on step sizes and noise. A validated endpoint method must address these effects.

R2. H1a/H1b are separate preparations of H1, giving 123.1107 and 124.1116 mg/L as CaCO₃. H2a is another supplied sample at 191.1719 mg/L. Two preparations of one water do not become two independently collected waters. Shared titrant concentration uncertainty or endpoint bias is not established from their difference.

R3. The corrected consumption is 0.00400 L; amount is \(4.00\times10^{-5}\) mol, equivalent to 4.0036 mg CaCO₃ in 0.02500 L. Result is 160.144 mg/L as CaCO₃:

new_hardness <- 0.01000 * ((4.10 - 0.10) / 1000) *
                  100.09 * 1000 / (25.00 / 1000)
new_hardness
