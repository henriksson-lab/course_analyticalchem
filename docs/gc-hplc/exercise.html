Exercise: GC, HPLC and internal-standard reasoning

Goal

Choose a plausible chromatographic route for a chemical question, calibrate integrated peak areas, and explain which shared effects an internal standard can compensate. Calculate first by hand; use the embedded experiment to test the assumptions; then analyse the supplied caffeine data in R.

Background

GC transports a vapour in a carrier gas; HPLC transports solute in a liquid. Introduction, stability, stationary-phase interactions and detector selectivity all belong to the method. A headspace GC method can sample volatiles above a nonvolatile matrix. A UV chromatogram still requires an absorbing analyte and adequate separation at the selected wavelength.

For this simulated HPLC case, final caffeine standards span 0–40 mg/L. Every standard and sample has 10 mg/L internal standard. Supplied integrated areas give these retained-intercept relationships:

\[A_{caffeine}=3+50c,\qquad R=A_{caffeine}/A_{IS}=0.006+0.100c.\]

The fixed internal-standard concentration is part of this calibration design. An internal standard can track only effects it experiences appropriately. A late addition cannot establish earlier extraction recovery, and a ratio cannot repair coelution or analyte-only matrix changes.

By hand

B1. Two injections of the same preparation give caffeine/IS areas 753/500 and 603/400. Calculate final concentration using external area and area ratio. The original-to-final dilution factor is 10. Report both sets on the original basis. Hint

B2. A new accepted injection gives areas 903/600, still with 10 mg/L IS and dilution factor 5. Calculate the original caffeine concentration using the ratio model. Hint

B3. Compare a volatile solvent mixture with an aqueous mixture of nonvolatile heat-sensitive compounds. Give a plausible GC/LC starting choice, and one condition that could change your decision. Hint

B4. A standard is added after extraction. Both resolved peaks share the same injection-volume variation, but only native caffeine lost 40% during extraction. Which effect can the ratio compensate? What additional assumption is needed if the standard is instead added before extraction? Hint

Explore

Predict before changing a control. The experiment varies injection, recovery, standard-addition timing and analyte-only matrix response while keeping a known simulation truth of 150 mg/L original caffeine. Constant offsets prevent perfect cancellation even in the otherwise matched model.

E1. At default recovery and matrix response, change injection multiplier from 0.8 to 1.0 and 1.2. Compare external and ratio estimates. Explain the small remaining ratio change rather than claiming exact invariance.

E2. Set injection multiplier to 1.0 and recovery to 60%. Compare IS added after and before extraction. Which condition is assumed when the early standard compensates? Does the constant offset disappear?

E3. Reset. Change only analyte response to 0.8, then separately introduce a known coeluting area of 100. Explain why neither effect is removed by an unchanged IS response. Which candidate remains calculable but is withheld?

Solutions — E1–E3

E1. At injection 0.8, external original is 120 mg/L while ratio original is 150.15 mg/L. At 1.0 both are150. At 1.2 external is 180 and ratio about 149.90. The common multiplicative injection factor mostly cancels in the ratio, but the constant caffeine-area offset3 does not scale with injection.

E2. At unit injection and 60% recovery, a late IS gives ratio original 90 mg/L: it never experienced the earlier loss. The early IS is assumed to have exactly the same recovery; its ratio original is about 150.40 mg/L, near the150 truth. The residual0.40 comes from the nonmultiplicative offset. Native/spiked material may behave differently in a real extraction, so early timing alone is not proof of equivalent recovery.

E3. With analyte-only response0.8 at the default injection 0.8, the ratio candidate is 120.15 mg/L, not150. With coeluting area 100 instead and all other settings reset, the candidate is 170.15 mg/L. Both selected model failures cause the original-concentration claim to be withheld: the ratio cannot supply missing chemical equivalence or selectivity. The candidate curves remain diagnostic.

In R

Run from the extracted project root. The supplied flags represent separation and QC review for this scenario, not switches to set after viewing a desired answer. lm() uses the familiar response-on-concentration direction.

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
standards <- read_csv("data/course/hplc-standards.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
injections <- read_csv("data/course/hplc-injections.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)

R1. Add area ratio to both tables. Fit external-area and ratio calibrations and calculate each injection’s final and original candidate concentration. Which two injections belong to the same preparation? Hint

R2. Require range, supplied resolution and QC to pass before exporting a reportable original result. Which two injections must be withheld, and why? Plot the repeated D1 estimates by calibration approach. Hint

R3. Export a demo curve and, if available, import it in R. Plot the external and ratio candidates against injection multiplier. Distinguish a curve position in a model sweep from an independent sample. Hint

Hints

Hint — B1 and B2

Invert each fitted relationship separately. Compute \(R\) before subtracting its intercept. Multiply final concentration by the original-to-final preparation factor only once.

Hint — B3

Ask how each analyte can enter the mobile phase and whether it survives the conditions. Matrix and detector requirements can change a plausible first choice.

Hint — B4

Trace the analyte and standard through extraction and injection separately. Earlier timing helps only if their behaviour through the relevant step is appropriately equivalent.

Hint — R1

Use mutate(area_ratio = caffeine_area / internal_standard_area). Fit the ratios with final caffeine as explanatory variable, keeping the intercept.

Hint — R2

Combine independent evidence with &. if_else() can assign NA_real_ to unsupported concentrations; preserve the reason and preparation ID as well.

Hint — R3

The CSV separates candidate_... columns from reported_original_mg_l. A blank reported value imports as missing, not zero caffeine.

Solutions

Solutions — B1–B4

B1. External final concentrations are \((753-3)/50=15.00\) and \((603-3)/50=12.00\) mg/L. Ratios are1.506 and 1.5075, giving15.000 and 15.015 mg/L final. Original results are150 and 120 mg/L externally, and 150.000 and 150.150 by ratio. Their near agreement reflects compensation for a shared injection effect, not independent sample replication.

B2. \(R=903/600=1.505\); \(c=(1.505-0.006)/0.100=14.99\) mg/L final; original is 74.95 mg/L. This assumes accepted selectivity, range and QC.

B3. GC is a plausible start for the volatile solvent mixture; LC for the nonvolatile heat-sensitive aqueous solutes. Solubility, thermal stability, headspace partitioning, matrix compatibility or detector response could refine that choice. Neither instrument is universally best.

B4. The ratio can track shared injection variation but not caffeine loss before the late IS addition. An early IS would need sufficiently equivalent recovery and response; freely added standard and native bound analyte need not behave identically.

Full worked R solutions — R1–R3

R1.

standards <- standards |>
  mutate(area_ratio = caffeine_area / internal_standard_area)
fit_external <- lm(caffeine_area ~ caffeine_mg_l, data = standards)
fit_ratio <- lm(area_ratio ~ caffeine_mg_l, data = standards)
injections <- injections |>
  mutate(area_ratio = caffeine_area / internal_standard_area,
         final_external_mg_l = (caffeine_area - coef(fit_external)[1]) /
                                coef(fit_external)[2],
         final_ratio_mg_l = (area_ratio - coef(fit_ratio)[1]) /
                             coef(fit_ratio)[2],
         candidate_external_original_mg_l = final_external_mg_l * dilution_factor,
         candidate_ratio_original_mg_l = final_ratio_mg_l * dilution_factor)
injections

The fits are area 3+50c and ratio 0.006+0.100c. I1 and I2 are repeated injections of D1. Their final ratio estimates 15.000 and 15.015 agree much better than their external estimates 15 and 12. They do not establish two independent original drinks.

R2.

results <- injections |>
  mutate(in_range = final_ratio_mg_l >= 0 & final_ratio_mg_l <= 40,
         reportable = in_range & resolved & qc_pass,
         original_mg_l = if_else(reportable,
                                candidate_ratio_original_mg_l, NA_real_),
         decision = case_when(!qc_pass ~ "withhold: QC failed",
                              !resolved ~ "withhold: coelution",
                              !in_range ~ "remeasure: outside range",
                              TRUE ~ "conditional result"))
write_csv(results, "output/hplc-decisions.csv", na = "NA")
comparison <- bind_rows(
  filter(results, preparation_id == "D1") |>
    transmute(injection_id, method = "External area", estimate = final_external_mg_l),
  filter(results, preparation_id == "D1") |>
    transmute(injection_id, method = "Area ratio", estimate = final_ratio_mg_l))
ggplot(comparison, aes(injection_id, estimate, colour = method, group = method)) +
  geom_point() + geom_line() +
  labs(x = "Repeated injection of D1", y = "Estimated final caffeine (mg/L)") +
  theme_minimal()

I3 is unresolved and I4 has failed QC. Both reportable concentration fields are missing even though an algebraic candidate can be calculated. No zero-caffeine claim follows from either failure.

R3. This optional block runs only after saving the exported file in data/:

if (file.exists("data/internal-standard-experiment.csv")) {
  demo <- read_csv("data/internal-standard-experiment.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
  ggplot(demo, aes(injection_multiplier, candidate_ratio_original_mg_l)) +
    geom_line() +
    geom_hline(yintercept = 150, linetype = "dashed") +
    labs(x = "Injection amount multiplier", y = "Ratio candidate original caffeine (mg/L)") +
    theme_minimal()
}

Changing multiplier walks through a deterministic model sweep; its rows are not additional sampled drinks. The explanatory controls encode known simulation effects. A real instrument does not automatically know native recovery or a coeluting contribution from its area ratio alone.
