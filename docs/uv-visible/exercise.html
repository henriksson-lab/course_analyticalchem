Exercise: UV and visible absorption

Goal

Connect an absorbing chemical species to a defensible iron concentration. Retrieve plotting and regression in R; extend these with keyed preparation joins and inverse-calibration uncertainty.

Background

How much dissolved iron is in the supplied filtered water, and which results are supported by the measurements? We will make a coloured iron complex, use its light absorption to estimate concentration, then check the preparation factor, calibration, and independent quality-control result.

Use your familiar R project, CSV, plotting, and straight-line skills. The new steps are matching preparation records by ID and accounting for uncertainty when converting a signal to concentration.

Download the complete project, extract it, and open analyticalchem.Rproj. Keep report.Rmd at the project root. The project needs R packages readr, dplyr, ggplot2, knitr, and rmarkdown. See the R reference for package setup.

All measurements here are simulated. This is an analysis exercise; use an approved practical protocol for any actual laboratory work.

Make an absorber we can measure

Iron(II) reacts with 1,10-phenanthroline, abbreviated phen, to form a red-orange complex:

\[\mathrm{Fe^{2+} + 3\,phen \rightleftharpoons [Fe(phen)_3]^{2+}}\]

Three bidentate ligands coordinate one iron ion. We measure the complex near 510 nm. Reduction maintains the iron(II) state; controlled acidity and excess ligand support colour formation. University laboratory background

For this exercise, assume quantitative reduction and complex formation and no iron loss. The measurand is mg Fe/L in the supplied filtered water, including iron converted to Fe(II) in the assumed preparation. It is not the original Fe(II) concentration before treatment. No evidence about particulate iron or digestion recovery is supplied.

Predict: if reduction is incomplete, will the result be biased high or low? Explain using the species that absorbs the light.

Connect the apparatus to absorbance

At a selected wavelength, light passes through a cuvette. The detector measures transmitted intensity \(I\) relative to reference intensity \(I_0\):

\[T = I/I_0, \qquad A = -\log_{10}(T)\]

A reagent blank contains the preparation reagents without the analyte and accounts for their background under matched conditions. Absorbance in these files is already referenced to a reagent blank. Do not blank-correct it again.

A transmittance of 0.50 corresponds to absorbance 0.301. If twice as much analyte doubles absorbance within the linear range, it does not halve transmittance: \(T=10^{-A}\).

For an appropriate dilute solution at fixed wavelength and path length, Beer–Lambert behaviour is \(A=\varepsilon l c\), with \(c\) in mol/L when using a molar absorptivity. Here our calibration uses mg of elemental Fe/L, so its slope has units L/mg Fe. Do not substitute that slope into a molar equation without converting the concentration basis.

B2. A line is \(A=0.004+0.199c\). An unknown gives \(A=0.4806\). Estimate final concentration and the original concentration for 10.00 mL made up to 50.00 mL. State the concentration basis. Hint

In R

R1. Choose the wavelength from a spectrum

Plot both traces. Explain the proposed wavelength and what one row represents. Hint

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
spectra <- read_csv("data/spectra.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
ggplot(spectra, aes(wavelength_nm, absorbance, colour = trace)) +
  geom_line() +
  labs(x = "Wavelength (nm)", y = "Absorbance", colour = "Solution") +
  theme_minimal()

The spectral shape supports choosing 510 nm for a strong response. A maximum also makes a small wavelength-setting error less consequential than on a steep spectral flank. It does not prove that other sample components cannot absorb. This simulated envelope is not a reference spectrum for identifying a compound.

Each row is a wavelength in one scan. These rows do not give hundreds of independently prepared standards.

R2. Fit the familiar calibration

Interpret slope and intercept with units and inspect the residual pattern. State what this diagnostic cannot establish about a sample matrix. Hint

standards <- read_csv("data/standards.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
fit <- lm(absorbance ~ standard_mg_l, data = standards)
coef(fit)
a <- unname(coef(fit)[1])
b <- unname(coef(fit)[2])
ggplot(standards, aes(standard_mg_l, absorbance)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_abline(intercept = a, slope = b) +
  labs(x = "Iron in final solution (mg Fe/L)",
       y = "Absorbance at 510 nm") +
  theme_minimal()

The fitted relationship is approximately \(A=0.003874+0.198799c\). The nonzero intercept is estimated, not forced to zero. Its existence alone does not identify the source of the offset.

The concentration is deliberately set; absorbance is the response. Fitting standard_mg_l ~ absorbance describes a different regression. Estimate an unknown using the inverse of our fitted response model:

\[\hat c_{final} = (\bar A_{unknown}-a)/b\]

standards <- standards |> mutate(residual = residuals(fit))
ggplot(standards, aes(standard_mg_l, residual)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_point() +
  labs(x = "Iron in final solution (mg Fe/L)",
       y = "Observed minus fitted absorbance") +
  theme_minimal()

Look for curvature, changing spread, and unusual observations. Small residuals on standards cannot establish recovery or selectivity in an unknown water. Do not remove a point simply to improve the fit.

R3. Match the measurement to its preparation

Check key uniqueness and matches, then explain why the deliberately reversed preparation-table order must not affect the join. Hint

P01 and P02 are separate preparations of W1, each read three times. We will first analyse P01. P03 and P04 belong to W2; P04 is a newly measured preparation with a smaller aliquot. P05 belongs to a later measurement block.

readings <- read_csv("data/readings.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
preparations <- read_csv("data/preparations.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
stopifnot(!anyDuplicated(preparations$preparation_id),
          all(readings$preparation_id %in% preparations$preparation_id))
means <- readings |>
  group_by(preparation_id) |>
  summarise(m = n(), mean_absorbance = mean(absorbance),
            .groups = "drop")
joined <- means |> left_join(preparations, by = "preparation_id")
joined

left_join() attaches the matching preparation information to each measurement summary using the ID. It does not assume that the files have the same row order. The checks require one preparation record per key and a match for every measured preparation. Inspect IDs, row count, units, and missing values after joining.

For P01, mean absorbance is 0.480620, corresponding to about 2.398 mg Fe/L in the final coloured solution.

R4. Return to the original water concentration

Calculate P01 on the original-water basis and explain which volume the correction uses. Hint

\[\hat c_{original}=\hat c_{final}\frac{V_{final}}{V_{aliquot}}\]

For P01, \(V_{final}=50.00\) mL and \(V_{aliquot}=10.00\) mL. Multiply by 5, giving about 11.99 mg Fe/L in the supplied water. The final volume is the total volume, not the amount of water added.

Predict: P04 uses 5.00 mL made up to 50.00 mL. Should its final concentration be multiplied by 5 or 10? Which recorded field tells you?

R5. Check range before reporting a number

Identify the unsupported old preparation and distinguish it from the newly measured dilution. Hint

For this exercise, require every repeated reading to lie between the fitted responses at the lowest and highest calibration concentrations. This is a supplied working-range rule, not a general validation of the method.

response_limits <- predict(fit, newdata = data.frame(standard_mg_l = c(0, 4)))
range_check <- readings |>
  group_by(preparation_id) |>
  summarise(in_range = all(absorbance >= response_limits[1] &
                          absorbance <= response_limits[2]), .groups = "drop")
range_check

P03 is outside range. Its old signal cannot be rescued by changing a dilution factor in the table. P04 is a new measurement of a new aliquot: its mean absorbance is 0.540610 and its dilution factor is 10. This gives about 27.00 mg Fe/L for W2 under the stated assumptions.

R6. Use an independent quality-control check

Apply the stated block checks and export only supported concentration estimates, retaining identifiers and reasons in your explanation. Hint

An independently sourced check solution contains 2.00 mg Fe/L in the final solution. Our exercise acceptance window is 95–105% recovery. It checks the calibration in block A and again before the later block B.

qc <- read_csv("data/qc.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
qc_summary <- qc |>
  group_by(block) |>
  summarise(mean_absorbance = mean(absorbance),
            reference_mg_l = first(reference_mg_l), .groups = "drop") |>
  mutate(estimated_mg_l = (mean_absorbance - a) / b,
         recovery_percent = 100 * estimated_mg_l / reference_mg_l,
         qc_pass = recovery_percent >= 95 & recovery_percent <= 105)
qc_summary

A recovers about 99.6%. B recovers about 85.2%. Withhold P05 from B, even though its signal is inside the calibration range. Investigate, correct the problem, establish satisfactory calibration and QC, and remeasure the sample. Do not automatically divide results by 0.852: the cause of the failure is not established. A passing simple check also does not prove that every sample matrix is free from interference.

Now combine the two checks so unsupported estimates cannot appear as results:

results <- joined |>
  left_join(range_check, by = "preparation_id") |>
  left_join(qc_summary |> select(block, qc_pass), by = "block") |>
  mutate(reportable = in_range & qc_pass,
         final_mg_l = if_else(reportable,
                              (mean_absorbance - a) / b, NA_real_),
         original_mg_l = final_mg_l * final_ml / aliquot_ml)
results |> select(preparation_id, sample_id, original_mg_l, reportable)
write_csv(results, "output/student-concentrations.csv", na = "NA")

Missing values here mean withheld analytical estimates, not zero iron. Include the reason for withholding in your report.

R7. Extend the earlier course with uncertainty

Calculate P01 standard uncertainty and distinguish it from the helper’s approximate 95% final-solution interval. State the omitted sources. Hint

Repeated readings do not remove uncertainty in the calibration or in the preparation volumes. A simple concentration estimate also needs uncertainty from the fitted slope and intercept. NIST calibration guidance

Run the supplied helper for P01; you do not need to write a function:

source("R/inverse-calibration.R")
p01_readings <- readings |> filter(preparation_id == "P01")
u_p01 <- inverse_calibration(fit, p01_readings$absorbance)
u_p01

It returns the point estimate, an approximate standard uncertainty from response and calibration, and an approximate 95% interval for final concentration. For P01, these are about 2.3981 mg Fe/L, 0.00451 mg Fe/L, and 2.3886–2.4077 mg Fe/L respectively. These are different summaries: standard uncertainty is not a 95% half-width.

The helper assumes a correct linear model, independent normal response errors with constant variance, negligible calibration-concentration uncertainty, and the same reading variance for standards and unknowns. It uses a first-order approximation appropriate for this well-determined slope. It does not validate those assumptions for another dataset. Forward predict() intervals alone are not concentration intervals; the helper propagates through the inverse relation.

Add independent supplied volume uncertainties using the relation below. Here \(u\) denotes standard uncertainty:

\[u(c_{original})=\sqrt{(D u(c_{final}))^2+ \left(c_{original}\frac{u(V_{final})}{V_{final}}\right)^2+ \left(c_{original}\frac{u(V_{aliquot})}{V_{aliquot}}\right)^2}\]

p01 <- results |> filter(preparation_id == "P01")
D <- p01$final_ml / p01$aliquot_ml
u_original <- sqrt(
  (D * u_p01$u_cal_mg_l)^2 +
  (p01$original_mg_l * p01$u_final_ml / p01$final_ml)^2 +
  (p01$original_mg_l * p01$u_aliquot_ml / p01$aliquot_ml)^2)
u_original

One suitable statement is: P01 gave 11.991 mg Fe/L with a standard uncertainty of 0.028 mg Fe/L from the stated calibration, reading, and volume model. This is not a full method uncertainty or a 95% interval. The budget excludes sampling, matrix bias, incomplete recovery, and uncertain standard concentrations. Keep full precision for calculations and round only reported values.

P01 and P02 share a calibration and come from the same collected sample. Do not average their uncertainty estimates as though they were independent calibrations, or describe six readings as six water samples.

R8. Your report and transfer task

Produce the report and calculate the new preparation below. Explain its reporting conditions as well as its concentration. Hint

  1. Render the supplied report.Rmd unchanged, then save an edited copy at the project root. Explain the absorber, wavelength, concentration basis, and assumed preparation chemistry.
  2. Add the spectral, calibration, and residual plots with your interpretations.
  3. Complete the ID join, range checks, and QC checks. Report P01, P02, and P04 separately and explain why P03 and P05 do not have reportable concentrations.
  4. Add P01’s uncertainty calculation and state the omissions. Explain why the SD of its three absorbances alone is insufficient.
  5. For a new preparation, a 2.00 mL aliquot was made up to 50.00 mL. Its three readings are 0.399, 0.401, and 0.400 in accepted block A. Estimate the original concentration. Compare its preparation factor with P01’s.
  6. Restart R and render using rmarkdown::render("report.Rmd", output_dir = "reports") from the console. Keep the source, input data, CSV results, and HTML report together.
Numerical checks

P01 ≈ 11.99 mg Fe/L, P02 ≈ 12.10 mg Fe/L, P04 ≈ 27.00 mg Fe/L. P03 needs remeasurement; P05 is withheld after QC failure. For the new preparation, \(\bar A=0.400\), \(D=25\), and the original concentration is approximately 49.81 mg Fe/L. This is a point estimate, not a complete uncertainty statement.

Check your reasoning
  • Incomplete reduction? Less absorbing Fe(II) complex can give a low result.
  • Why not subtract the blank again? The supplied absorbances already use it as the reference. A further subtraction would change the measurement basis.
  • Why is a good line insufficient? Sample range, recovery, selectivity, preparation, and current QC still matter.
  • Does a QC failure identify its cause? No. It signals that results need to be withheld and the measurement process investigated.
  • Can more scans replace more samples? No. Wavelength positions and repeated readings are not independently collected waters.

Selected textbook reading: Harris and Lucy, topics on molecular absorption, spectrophotometers, calibration, and experimental uncertainty. Read by topic; the edition for the planned course remains to be confirmed.

Hints

B1: invert the logarithm

Use \(A=-\log_{10}T\), then \(T=10^{-A}\). Doubling the exponent squares the transmittance; it does not subtract a fixed fraction.

B2: final before original

Rearrange the fitted response as \(c=(A-a)/b\). The preparation factor is final total volume divided by sample aliquot, with matching volume units.

B3: evidence gates are separate

An in-range response does not repair a failed independent QC condition.

B4: inspect the concentration unit

Use \(c=A/(\varepsilon l)\). A coefficient per mol/L requires concentration in mol/L; the empirical iron line instead uses mg elemental Fe/L.

E1: hold other optical effects fixed

In the stray-free model, doubled path doubles absorbance and squares transmittance. With an unchanged cell/wavelength calibration, interpret the candidate separately from the matching and range decisions.

E2: sensitivity versus concentration

The illustrative envelope has lower sensitivity at 450 nm. Compare the new response with standards recalibrated under the same wavelength and path.

E3: inspect only the standard rows

A response floor is nonlinear. Fit absorbance ~ concentration_mg_l only after filtering the export to record_type == "standard"; sample truth is not a calibration standard. The optional import in the solution is guarded so your ordinary R workflow still runs without a downloaded CSV.

R1: row meaning

A wavelength position within one scan is not an independently prepared solution. A spectral maximum improves response but does not prove chemical specificity.

R2: measured response direction

lm fits absorbance on known concentration. Look for curvature and changing spread before inverting; retaining an intercept does not establish the physical cause of an offset.

R3: key matching

left_join uses IDs, not row positions. Unique preparation keys avoid duplicated measurement rows; complete matches avoid losing the correct dilution volumes.

R4: return through the volume chain

P01’s mean absorbance is 0.480620. Use the fitted coefficients at full precision, then multiply by 50.00/10.00. Final volume includes reagents and solvent.

R5: old reading versus new evidence

P03’s signal exceeds the standard range. P04 has its own measurement and a new 5.00 mL aliquot; changing the number attached to P03 would not supply that evidence.

R6: retain the failure reason

if_else() leaves unsupported estimates as NA_real_. Block A and later B have different check evidence; failed B does not retrospectively invalidate A.

R7: identify the scale and coverage

The helper’s final-concentration standard uncertainty, its approximate 95% interval and the combined original-concentration standard uncertainty are three different summaries. Do not use one name for all of them.

R8: transfer the measured preparation

The new readings average 0.400. Its aliquot is 2.00 mL, not P01’s 10.00 mL; its factor is 50.00/2.00. Use accepted block A and the stated assay assumptions.

Solutions

By hand and optical exploration

B1. \(A=-\log_{10}(0.50)=0.30103\). Doubling gives 0.60206, so \(T=10^{-0.60206}=\mathbf{0.25}\). This happens to equal half of 0.50, but the general relationship is squaring, not halving. Starting instead at 0.80 gives \(A=0.09691\) and doubled absorbance gives \(T=0.80^2=\mathbf{0.64}\), not 0.40.

B2. Final concentration is \((0.4806-0.004)/0.199=\mathbf{2.39497}\) mg Fe/L. The original-water factor is \(50.00/10.00=5\), giving 11.9749 mg Fe/L. The slightly rounded hand line differs from the later fitted data line; neither answer refers to mg complex/L.

B3. Withhold the affected later-block sample despite an in-range signal. Investigate, establish satisfactory calibration/checks, and remeasure. The 85.2% recovery does not identify the failure cause or establish a valid universal correction by division.

B4. \(c=0.600/(2000\times1.00)=\mathbf{3.00\times10^{-4}}\) mol/L. This is a separate supplied absorber example. The iron slope in L/mg Fe uses an elemental mass concentration; a molar absorptivity uses mol/L and a stated path. Conversion of the concentration basis is required before comparing their numerical values.

E1. At reset, input is 2.4 mg Fe/L. Doubling path doubles ideal absorbance from 0.47712 to 0.95424 and squares transmission. Recalibration at the selected path still returns 2.400 mg Fe/L. With recalibration off, the old 1 cm line gives a candidate 4.800 mg Fe/L: the demo withholds it because cell conditions differ and the candidate exceeds the prepared 0–4 mg/L range. A candidate is not made reportable merely because an algebraic inverse exists.

E2. Sensitivity and absorbance decrease; transmission increases. The matched stray-free calibration returns the same 2.400 mg Fe/L concentration in this noise-free model. It cannot establish real precision or selectivity at the new wavelength.

E3. Unabsorbed stray light creates a transmission floor. The underlying response bends down relative to Beer–Lambert even after fitting a straight line to the standards. A small departure might remain within the exercise’s 0.005-absorbance check; a larger one fails it. Passing that threshold is not a complete method validation. To reproduce exported standard residuals, save the CSV in output/uv-visible-demo.csv and optionally run:

demo_path <- "output/uv-visible-demo.csv"
if (file.exists(demo_path)) {
  demo <- read_csv(demo_path, show_col_types = FALSE)
  demo_standards <- demo |> filter(record_type == "standard")
  demo_fit <- lm(absorbance ~ concentration_mg_l, data = demo_standards)
  demo_standards <- demo_standards |> mutate(residual = residuals(demo_fit))
  ggplot(demo_standards, aes(concentration_mg_l, residual)) +
    geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed") + geom_point() +
    labs(x = "Prepared standard (mg Fe/L)", y = "Residual absorbance") +
    theme_minimal()
}
R1–R8: full analysis and reporting interpretation

R1. The standard has its illustrative maximum near 510 nm and the reagent blank is small. A strong local maximum supports sensitivity and reduces the first-order effect of a small wavelength-setting error relative to a steep flank. It does not show that the sample has no other absorbing component. Rows are positions in one spectral scan, not many independent standards.

R2. The fitted intercept is about 0.003873883, slope 0.198799368 L/mg Fe. Residuals are small without an obvious curved pattern in these simulated standards. Retain the intercept rather than force zero. The residual pattern neither identifies the offset’s cause nor establishes recovery/selectivity in an unknown sample matrix. Reversing the regression is a different fit.

R3. The key checks establish one preparation record per ID and a match for every measured preparation. left_join(..., by = "preparation_id") therefore attaches the correct aliquot, final volume and block despite reversed table order. P01/P02 are two preparations of W1, each with three repeated readings; they are not six separately collected waters.

R4. P01 mean absorbance is 0.480620. Inverting the fitted line gives 2.3981269 mg Fe/L final, then multiplying by five gives 11.9906346 mg Fe/L original. This uses total final volume, including all reagents and solvent.

R5. P03 is outside range and needs appropriate new measurement. P04 is a new 5.00 mL aliquot made to 50.00 mL, measured at mean absorbance 0.540610. Its factor is ten, giving 26.9988844 mg Fe/L original. It does not validate the old P03 reading by retroactive relabelling.

R6. Block A recovers 99.647% and passes the supplied 95–105% rule; later B recovers 85.176% and fails. P01, P02 and P04 are supported under the stated assumptions, at about 11.99, 12.10 and 27.00 mg Fe/L original. P03/P05 are withheld for range/QC respectively. Their NA concentrations are not zero analyte. A passing check still does not establish every sample’s matrix behaviour or original digestion recovery.

R7. P01 final-concentration standard uncertainty is 0.00450904 mg Fe/L; its approximate 95% interval is 2.388568–2.407686 mg Fe/L final under the helper’s independent normal, equal-variance response model, negligible standard- concentration uncertainty and well-determined positive slope. Scaling the standard uncertainty by dilution and combining the independent volume terms produces 0.0276962 mg Fe/L original. A suitable statement is 11.991 mg Fe/L with standard uncertainty 0.028 mg Fe/L under this limited model, not a 95% half-width. Sampling, matrix bias, incomplete native recovery and standard- concentration uncertainty remain omitted. Shared calibration means uncertainty from two preparations must not be averaged as though both calibrations were independent.

R8. The separate new preparation averages 0.400. Its factor is 25:

transfer_final_mg_l <- (mean(c(0.399, 0.401, 0.400)) - a) / b
transfer_original_mg_l <- transfer_final_mg_l * 50.00 / 2.00
transfer_original_mg_l

The point estimate is 49.81481 mg Fe/L original, conditional on the accepted block, matching chemistry and in-range new reading. Its own preparation uncertainties are not supplied, so the point answer does not establish a full uncertainty. The final report should retain raw files, preparation IDs, model, plots, withheld reasons and rendered HTML, and run again from a fresh R session.
