Measuring iron with visible light

Chemistry, calibration, and a defensible result

What number are we trying to report?

From the supplied water sample through preparation, calibration, and checking to a reported concentration.

mg elemental Fe/L in the supplied filtered water. What must be true before that number is meaningful?

Ask students to distinguish a signal from a chemical result. The waters are simulated, filtered and fortified. Assume all dissolved iron is quantitatively reduced and complexed, with no loss. This is not a digestion or total-recovery claim for arbitrary environmental water. The full unit and project are linked from the course home page.

Chemistry creates the absorber

Fe²⁺ plus three phenanthroline ligands forms the red-orange iron complex.

Reduction, acidity, and excess ligand affect the amount of coloured complex.

Phen means 1,10-phenanthroline. Each ligand is bidentate. The diagram illustrates stoichiometry, not geometry. Ask what incomplete reduction would do to the result. Do not equate measured iron after reduction with original Fe(II). Background: https://staff.buffalostate.edu/nazareay/che301/l9.pdf .

Follow the light

Selected light enters a one-centimetre cuvette. The detector measures the transmitted light relative to the reference.

The reference is a reagent blank. These files are already blank-referenced.

I zero is the matched reference intensity; I is transmitted intensity through the prepared sample. Discuss the purpose of a reagent blank and why a further subtraction changes an already referenced measurement. Compare an empty cell, solvent reference, and a matched reagent blank verbally.

Why choose 510 nm?

The simulated iron-standard spectrum peaks near 510 nm and the reagent-blank response is small.

A strong response and a flat local maximum help. Does the maximum prove selectivity?

It does not: other components may absorb. These curves are a simulated Gaussian envelope, not authentic identification spectra. Rows are wavelengths within a scan, not independent replicates. Connect to the known approximate visible maximum of the iron(II) complex; the university example uses 508 nm.

Absorbance and concentration

\[A=-\log_{10}(I/I_0), \qquad A=\varepsilon l c\]

At fixed wavelength and path, a suitable dilute solution gives a proportional response. Our calibration uses mg Fe/L, not mol/L.

Predict: if \(T=0.50\), \(A\approx0.301\). If absorbance doubles, what happens to transmittance?

Transmittance becomes approximately 0.25. Emphasise that doubling absorbance squares transmittance. We use an empirical calibration slope in L/mg Fe, not a molar absorptivity. Chemical equilibrium, stray light, and excessive absorbance can break a simple linear relationship.

Fit what we measured

Standards between zero and four mg Fe/L and their fitted absorbance response.

lm(absorbance ~ standard_mg_l, data = standards)

Retrieve the earlier calibration lesson. Eighteen independent final standard solutions, three per concentration, are measured once each. The unweighted line retains its intercept: a=0.003873883 and b=0.198799368. Do not force zero or reverse the regression. Units of slope are L/mg Fe.

A good line needs a diagnostic

Residual absorbance versus concentration; no obvious curvature in this teaching dataset.

What would curvature mean? What could small residuals still fail to reveal?

Inspect curvature, spread and unusual points. The assumptions are constant response variance and negligible standard concentration uncertainty. Small residuals cannot validate recovery or sample-specific interferences. Do not remove inconvenient observations simply to improve the fit.

What counts as replication?

One collected water sample gives two independent preparations, each measured three times.

Six readings are not six independently collected waters.

P01 and P02 share W1. Repeated readings describe reading repeatability; preparing another aliquot probes additional variation. Both still use the same calibration. Do not divide every contribution by the square root of six.

Correct the preparation factor

Ten millilitres made up to a total of fifty millilitres gives a factor of five.

P01: \(\bar A=0.480620\) → 2.398 mg Fe/L final → 11.99 mg Fe/L original.

Invert A=a+bc, then multiply by final volume over aliquot volume. Total final volume includes reagents. Keep all numerical precision in code. State the concentration basis: elemental iron in the original supplied filtered water.

An old out-of-range reading needs new evidence

P03 lies beyond the standard range; the newly prepared and measured P04 lies within it.

P04 uses a new 5.00 mL aliquot, made to 50.00 mL. Its result is about 27.00 mg Fe/L original.

P03 mean A=1.07507 exceeds the validated teaching range. P04 mean A=0.54061 is within range; its factor is 10. Editing the dilution factor on P03 cannot create a new measurement. Independent QC can stop reporting

Independent QC recovers about 99.6 percent in block A and 85.2 percent in later block B; only A meets the exercise criterion.

Withhold P05 from block B. Investigate and remeasure after satisfactory QC.

Acceptance is a supplied 95–105 percent recovery window for this exercise. Calibration from A is checked before use in later B. Do not retroactively invalidate A or automatically correct B by dividing by the failed QC recovery. QC failure signals a problem but does not identify the cause.

Uncertainty survives averaging

Illustrative P01 standard uncertainty contributions: calibration and readings, final volume, and aliquot volume.

P01: 11.991 mg Fe/L; standard uncertainty 0.028 mg Fe/L under the stated model.

The supplied helper includes response and slope/intercept uncertainty through a first-order inverse-calibration calculation. Independent volume uncertainties are added by root-sum-of-squares. This is not a 95 percent half-width. Sampling, recovery, matrix bias and standard concentration uncertainty are omitted. The approximate 95 percent interval 2.3886–2.4077 applies only to final solution concentration before volume uncertainty is added.

Transfer the reasoning

  2. Estimate the final concentration.
  3. Apply the preparation factor.
  4. State what is missing from a full uncertainty statement.
