Exercise: calibration and quality control

Goal

Fit and inspect a response calibration, invert it with its concentration basis intact, distinguish range from independent QC and residual review, and interpret a conditional inverse uncertainty interval. Use the chemistry and design to justify the report before exporting a number.

Background

For a linear response \(y=a+bc+e\), set known concentration c and measure y. An unknown point estimate is \((\bar y-a)/b\), not a reversed regression. Retain the intercept unless a justified measurement model specifies otherwise. A good \(R^2\) cannot rule out curvature, matrix effects, drift or preparation loss.

The supplied iron calibration uses mg elemental Fe/L in a final coloured solution and absorbance already referenced to a reagent blank. A method blank, independent check and sample spike answer different questions. All data are simulated.

By hand

B1. A line is \(y=0.010+0.200c\), with c in mg/L final. A mean unknown response 0.510 follows a fivefold preparation dilution. Calculate final and original concentration. Hint

B2. Someone forces that line through zero while keeping slope 0.200. What estimate results from response 0.510? Why is concentration zero not sufficient reason to force a measured response through zero? Hint

B3. At c=2 mg/L, the line in B1 predicts 0.410. An observation is 0.440. Calculate the residual and explain its sign. Would one residual identify an instrumental cause? Hint

B4. An independently sourced 2.00 mg/L check is estimated as 1.70 mg/L. The preselected exercise window is 95–105% recovery. Calculate recovery and the action for affected unknowns. Hint

B5. In a separate ideal low-level model, blank SD is 0.004 signal units and sensitivity 0.500 signal units per mg/L. Calculate the rough 3-SD concentration increment. Is it a universally validated detection limit or proof of zero below that value? Hint

Explore

The demo uses a monotonic toy response \(y=0.010+0.200c/(1+qc)\) plus independent response noise, with q in L/mg. q=0 is linear; positive q gives a saturating curve. Standards, later sample and independent check are separate records. The known truth is visible only because this is a simulation; it does not appear in real unknown data.

E1. Set q=0 and response noise to zero. Why is a sample result withheld while residual review is pending? Accept the line after inspecting it, and check the 5 mg/L original unknown at dilution factor 2. Hint

E2. Set largest standard to 8 mg/L and q to 0.015 L/mg, retaining zero noise. Compare \(R^2\) with residual structure. What would rejecting the line mean, and what evidence would a narrower proposed range still need? Hint

E3. Reset to a linear response, largest standard 4 mg/L and accepted review. Set original unknown to 20 mg/L and dilution factor 1. Then make a new simulated measurement at factor 10. Explain why this is different from editing the preparation factor on the old response. Hint

E4. With an in-range sample, change later sensitivity by −20%. Which check sees this failure? Can the old standards’ residuals diagnose later drift? Hint

In R

Run from the supplied project root. These are the reviewed iron data, separate from the toy saturating-response demo. They cover 0–4 mg Fe/L in final solution.

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
standards <- read_csv("data/standards.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
fit <- lm(absorbance ~ standard_mg_l, data = standards)
a <- unname(coef(fit)[1])
b <- unname(coef(fit)[2])
standards <- standards |>
  mutate(fitted = fitted(fit), residual = residuals(fit))
ggplot(standards, aes(standard_mg_l, residual)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed") + geom_point() +
  labs(x = "Final iron concentration (mg Fe/L)", y = "Residual absorbance") +
  theme_minimal()
unknown <- c(.47922, .48041, .48223)
limits <- predict(fit, newdata = data.frame(standard_mg_l = c(0, 4)))
in_range <- all(unknown >= limits[1] & unknown <= limits[2])
candidate_final <- if (in_range) (mean(unknown) - a) / b else NA_real_
candidate_final
source("R/inverse-calibration.R")
conditional_interval <- inverse_calibration(fit, unknown)
conditional_interval
dir.create("output", showWarnings = FALSE)
write_csv(conditional_interval, "output/calibration-unknown.csv")

R1. Interpret a and b with units, inspect the residuals, and state the final concentration estimate. Why is it not yet an original-water result? Hint

R2. Interpret the helper’s standard uncertainty and approximate 95% interval. Name its assumptions and at least two omitted sources. Explain why a forward response interval is not itself a concentration interval. Hint

R3. Fit the deliberately curved dataset and plot its residuals. Explain what a model/range review would need, without deleting inconvenient standards merely to improve the graph. Hint

curved <- read_csv("data/course/nonlinear-calibration.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)

Hints

Hint B1

Subtract the fitted intercept before dividing by slope. Then reverse the preparation dilution to return to the original basis.

Hint B2

Compare \(0.510/0.200\) with \((0.510-0.010)/0.200\). A response can have a background offset even at nominal concentration zero.

Hint B3

Residual is observed minus fitted. The sign locates a point relative to a line; it does not identify a chemical or instrumental mechanism by itself.

Hint B4

Recovery is \(100\times c_{estimated}/c_{reference}\). Apply the preselected rule before deciding on a reportable result.

Hint B5

Use \(3s/b\). A complete detection procedure also needs its noise, blank, matrix and decision-probability conditions.

Hints E1–E4

Range, independent QC and residual review are separate requirements. A simulator can expose the generating truth, but a real unknown cannot be validated merely by looking at a hidden correct answer.

Hint R1

lm() fits absorbance as response. Prepared concentration is the explanatory variable. Volume/preparation records connect final solution to original sample.

Hint R2

The helper propagates reading noise and the fitted intercept/slope covariance through an inverse relation. It excludes preparation and recovery effects.

Hint R3

residuals() can show curvature despite a high overall association. A justified range needs adequate standards and checks for the intended sample responses.

Hint R4

Including the check in calibration sacrifices its independent role. Filter by role, not by row position or whichever rows make the result look best.

Solutions

Solutions B1–B5

B1. \((0.510-0.010)/0.200=2.50\) mg/L final; original concentration is \(2.50\times5=12.5\) mg/L. Both units and material stage belong in the report.

B2. A forced-zero calculation gives 2.55 mg/L final, 2% above 2.50 mg/L. A nominal zero concentration does not guarantee a precisely zero response. The intercept must follow the justified model and evidence, not convenience.

B3. \(0.440-0.410=+0.030\) signal units. The point lies above the fitted line. One residual alone does not establish its cause; a pattern across concentration may support investigation of model failure.

B4. Recovery is \(100(1.70/2.00)=85.0\%\), failing the supplied 95–105% rule. Withhold affected unknowns, investigate, restore satisfactory calibration/checks and remeasure as appropriate. Dividing all results by 0.85 would assume a cause not established by the failed check.

B5. \(3(0.004)/0.500=0.024\) mg/L is a rough blank-relative concentration increment. It is not universal validation, and a below-limit response is not proof of exactly zero analyte.

Solutions E1–E4

E1. A model review has not yet been made, so a numerical candidate is not automatically reportable. With q=0, noise=0, accepted review and satisfactory range/QC, a=0.010 and b=0.200 give 2.50 mg/L final and 5.00 mg/L original.

E2. Positive q gives diminishing sensitivity and systematic residual structure despite high \(R^2\). Rejecting the line withholds the simple estimate. A narrower range may reduce nonlinearity, but needs adequate standards, diagnostics, independent checks and suitable intended sample responses; it is not validated by removing inconvenient points.

E3. At factor 1 the final concentration is 20 mg/L, beyond the four mg/L calibration. A new factor-10 measurement gives 2 mg/L final and returns to 20 mg/L original. It supplies a new observed response; changing a number beside the old out-of-range response supplies no new measurement.

E4. Under the linear zero-noise model a −20% sensitivity change gives an independent-check recovery of 80%, so affected samples are withheld. The old calibration residuals still look perfect because they predate the change.

Solutions R1–R4

R1. a≈0.003873883 absorbance units; b≈0.198799368 L/mg Fe. Final estimate is 2.3981269 mg Fe/L. The residuals lack obvious curvature in this selected range, though that does not prove sample recovery or selectivity. Original-water concentration also needs the actual aliquot and final-volume factor.

R2. The supplied helper gives standard uncertainty about 0.0045090 mg Fe/L and approximate 95% interval 2.388568–2.407686 mg Fe/L final. It assumes a correct linear model, independent normal equal-variance response errors, negligible calibration-concentration uncertainty and a well-determined positive slope. Sampling, volume uncertainty, matrix bias and recovery are omitted. A forward interval has response units; inverse concentration limits require the uncertain inverse relation, including intercept/slope covariance. The helper uses a first-order approximation, not a general nonlinear inverse algorithm.

R3. Curved-response residuals show a systematic concentration pattern. This is evidence against the single straight-line model over that full range, not permission to erase standards until \(R^2\) looks satisfactory. A proposed range or model needs independent evidence and fit to the analytical purpose.

R4. filter(role == "standard") reproduces the fitted coefficients within numerical rounding. The check material tests whether the selected calibration predicts an independently known quantity; fitting it into the same line would reduce its ability to reveal failure. Unknown rows do not supply known calibration concentrations in an actual analysis.

Where this leads

UV/visible absorption supplies the absorbing species, measurement path and preparation chemistry behind the iron response. All later methods revisit range, independent checks and honest uncertainty statements.
